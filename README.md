## How it learns

The idea comes from AlphaZero ([Silver et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar6404)): one general algorithm that learns from self-play alone. Each Pokémon generation is basically a new game for the same algorithm. The only things I change per generation are the rules, the input encoding, and a few training and search settings.

- **Self-play.** Each agent learns only from games against itself and its past versions.
- **PPO.** A small policy-and-value network (about 3M parameters), trained with proximal policy optimization ([Schulman et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)). My starting recipe came from [Wang (2024)](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/153888).
- **Search only at play time.** On the ladder, a short look-ahead search guided by the network's own policy and value picks each move. Gen 9's #1 seat also carried a hand-written loop breaker against repeated switching; it never triggered.

### Strength against Foul Play

I used [Foul Play](https://github.com/pmariglia/foul-play) for my offline evals, to measure each model's strength before putting it on the ladder. It's an open-source search bot that won the generation-9 OU track of the NeurIPS 2025 [PokéAgent Challenge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15563). Here's each model's win rate against it, with play-time search off and on:

| Gen | Without search | With search | Battles per column |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 71.0% ± 1.6 | 71.2% ± 1.6 | 3,000 |
| 2 | 69.9% ± 5.6 | 80.9% ± 4.8 | 256 |
| 4 | 61.6% ± 2.2 | 67.5% ± 2.0 | 2,000 |
| 6 | 66.4% ± 5.8 | 68.4% ± 5.7 | 256 |
| 7 | 66.8% ± 5.8 | 71.9% ± 5.5 | 256 |
| 9 | 66.7% ± 1.7 | not measured | 3,000 |

<sub>± is half the width of the 95% Wilson interval. Compare within a row only: Foul Play's search budget is fixed per generation. For gens 2, 4 and 6 these games used a smaller search than the ladder did. Gen 9's row is its #1 model; its search column was not measured.</sub>

### Post-optimization training cost

After the campaign I spent a few days speeding up the training pipeline. The learning algorithm didn't change.

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Games per hour | about 170,000 | about 630,000 |
| Cost per million games | about $10 | about $2 |

Before is the average of the runs above. After is gen 9 on one rented GPU, with a network three times as large as the one that reached #1. At these rates, a run the size of gen 9's #1 (about 50M games) now costs about $100 and takes about 3 days.

## How it differs from chess and Go

Most people know game-playing RL from chess and Go. Pokémon random battles are a different kind of game:

- **Hidden information.** You can't see the opponent's team, moves, items or abilities until they're revealed.
- **Chance.** Damage rolls, accuracy, critical hits and side effects make every turn random.
- **Simultaneous moves.** Both players pick at the same time, so being predictable gets punished.
- **Random teams.** Every game starts from a new, randomly dealt matchup (chess and Go always start from the same position).

## Prior work

- **[AlphaZero](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar6404)** (Silver et al., 2018) mastered chess, shogi and Go with one self-play algorithm, searching during training as well as play.
- **[Wang (2024)](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/153888)** and **[Foul Play](https://pmariglia.github.io/posts/foul-play/)** (pmariglia, 2025) reported peak ranks of 8 and 3 on the gen 4 random-battle ladder.
- **The [PokéAgent Challenge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15563)** (Karten et al., 2026), a NeurIPS 2025 competition, was played in gen 1 OU and gen 9 OU on a separate server for AI agents, with no public-ladder rank.
- **Earlier #1s.** To my knowledge, every earlier AI #1 was in gen 9, including Jaxcalibur's. None reached #1 in gens 1, 2, 4, 6 or 7, or in more than one generation.

## FAQ

**Did it use any human data?**<br>
No. No replays, no imitation of any player or bot, no scripted opponents.

**How long did it take?**<br>
I started on July 25, 2026. Gen 1 (the original goal) reached #1 66 days later, five #1s came by October 24, and gen 9's on November 7.

**What did it run on?**<br>
Gen 1 trained on my laptop. For each later generation I rented one GPU for a few days to speed things up. On the ladder, the network and all of its search ran on my laptop's CPU.

**What did it cost?**<br>
About $2,120 of rented GPU time for the whole project through November 7, 2026, dead ends and tests included. The [Training cost](#training-cost) table covers only each final network's run.

**How was it benchmarked before laddering?**<br>
Against Foul Play, at a fixed search budget (see [Strength against Foul Play](#strength-against-foul-play)).

**Did it follow Showdown's bot rules?**<br>
Yes. Every account has "bot" in its name and never posed as human.
- Gens 1 and 4: #1 before Showdown had a bot policy (its bot registry opened 2026-10-13).
- Gens 2 and 6: registered bots, before the bot opt-in (2026-10-20); matched like any other account.
- Gens 7 and 9: after the opt-in; only opted-in players and other bots.

**How does this relate to Jaxcalibur?**<br>
[Jaxcalibur](https://jaxcalibur.github.io/) reached #1 in gen 9 in August 2026 with self-play PPO. My gen 1 agents were already on the ladder then, and the last one built before its write-up reached #5. The write-up showed search in training wasn't needed, so I dropped it and adopted several of its ideas.

**Will you release the code or weights?**<br>
Not for now. Showdown's bot policy is new, and I'd rather not make it easier to run strong bots that don't follow it.
